MemReward: Graph-Based Experience Memory
for LLM Reward Prediction with Limited Labels

Abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning improves large language model (LLM) reasoning using reward signals on sampled rollouts. When reward labels are available for only a subset of rollouts, restricting policy updates to that subset leaves the remaining rollouts unused for policy optimization. Semi-supervised learning propagates labels from labeled to unlabeled samples, but existing methods either diffuse labels with fixed similarity weights or treat each sample independently, ignoring the relations among queries, thinking processes, and answers. We propose MemReward, a graph-based experience memory that propagates rewards to unlabeled rollouts during online policy optimization. MemReward stores queries and the rollouts (thinking processes and final answers) of an initial LLM policy as nodes of a heterogeneous graph connected by similarity and structural edges, and warms up a GNN on labeled rollouts to propagate rewards over this graph. During online RL fine-tuning, unlabeled rollouts are attached to the graph by query similarity, and the policy is updated with ground-truth rewards on labeled rollouts and GNN-predicted rewards on the rest. We evaluate MemReward on 13 benchmarks in mathematics, question answering, and code generation with verifiable rewards, and further with LLM-judge, human-preference, and continuous rewards. With ground-truth rewards on only 20% of rollouts, MemReward achieves 96.6% of the performance of fully supervised training (Oracle) on Qwen2.5-1.5B and 97.3% on Qwen2.5-3B, where it also outperforms label propagation and FixMatch-style self-training. It carries over to Llama backbones up to 8B and closely approaches Oracle on out-of-domain tasks.

Our contributions are:

  • Graph-based experience memory captures relations for reward prediction. We store queries, thinking processes and answers in a heterogeneous graph linked by similarity and structural edges. A graph neural network (GNN) learns to predict rewards by aggregating information through different relation types across mathematics, question answering and code generation.
  • Unlabeled rollouts contribute to online policy optimization. We connect new unlabeled queries to the stored graph by query similarity, then update the policy with ground-truth rewards on labeled rollouts and GNN-predicted rewards on the rest.

For each query with a ground-truth label, across math, question answering and code, the initial policy generates rollouts, each a thinking process and a final answer. The query, thinking process and answer are encoded into embeddings, and the rollouts are stored as experience memory.

Why is the model that turns each text into an embedding, the sentence encoder, kept fixed instead of being trained on rewards?

The sentence encoder all-MiniLM-L6-v2 is an implementation choice for representing queries and constructing candidate graph edges, not the focus of our method. Our contribution is not to optimize this encoder, but to study whether graph-based reward propagation can reliably exploit a fixed, generic semantic neighborhood, the queries a general-purpose model finds closest in meaning, to enhance RL training. The relevant question is therefore not whether every similarity edge is individually optimal, but whether the constructed graph contains sufficient signal that carries over to new data and whether the graph neural network (GNN) can aggregate useful relations while suppressing noisy ones. When the LLM being trained, the backbone, changes from Qwen to Llama, this encoder and all other components stay unchanged, and the consistent improvements on both Llama backbones indicate that the similarity graph and learned reward propagation remain effective across the models that generate the rollouts.

The graph has query, thinking and answer nodes, joined by three edge types. Query-query edges link a query to its top-k most similar queries by cosine similarity, query-thinking edges link a query to its thinking nodes, and thinking-answer edges pair each thinking node with its answer. Only query-query edges depend on embedding similarity.

Two similar queries can differ in whether their answers are right. Why connect queries by similarity at all?

We do not assume that semantic similarity directly determines whether an answer is right, or that every similarity edge links experiences with consistent rewards. Instead, we hypothesize that related queries often share reasoning requirements, solution strategies and failure modes, which gives a useful but potentially noisy neighborhood over which the GNN can learn reward-relevant relations. MemReward also does not predict rewards from query similarity alone: similarity is used only to pick related experiences, while the final reward prediction jointly uses the query, thinking and answer nodes. The GNN can therefore learn to emphasize informative neighbors and suppress semantically similar but reward-inconsistent ones.

A thinking text is far longer than its query. Why does that length not skew which experiences get connected?

Our graph does not place query, thinking and answer texts in a shared embedding space and connect all three node types by similarity. Thinking and answer nodes do not take part in the step that picks neighbors, retrieval; they join through fixed structural edges within each rollout, so the length of a thinking text does not determine which experiences are connected. A shared wording pattern, a template, could introduce spurious similarity if the three texts were concatenated into one node, but this is not how MemReward builds its graph: thinking and answer representations are used only after graph construction, in the step where each node updates its embedding from its neighbors, called message passing, and in reward prediction. We also tested the length effect directly: longer thinking is not systematically more similar to that of the seven nearest neighbors, with a correlation of −0.287 overall, negative in 7 of 9 benchmark-level measurements.

Why not give every edge a direction and forbid loops, which would make the graph a directed acyclic graph (DAG)?

A DAG is technically possible: query-query edges could be oriented by rollout generation time, label confidence, problem difficulty or a labeled-to-unlabeled propagation order, while keeping the query, thinking, answer hierarchy. However, semantic similarity between experiences does not naturally define a global partial order. Enforcing acyclicity would therefore require an additional hand-picked rule that the data do not supply, a heuristic, and could remove useful two-way relations or restrict information propagation between related experiences. We consequently use the current heterogeneous graph, which preserves the available relational evidence and lets the GNN learn how much each relation matters.

A heterogeneous GNN aggregates over each edge type with its own learnable weights, then scores each rollout with a scaled dot product between the query and rollout representations. It is trained on labeled rollouts with binary cross-entropy, or with a regression loss for continuous rewards such as token-level F1.

Why train a GNN instead of spreading labels over the same graph with a fixed rule?

To make the comparison controlled, a classical method that spreads labels from labeled to unlabeled samples over a graph, Label Propagation, uses exactly the same query embeddings, similarity graph, labeled examples and mixed-reward procedure of the RL algorithm that updates the policy, GRPO, as MemReward. The only difference is that it uses fixed label diffusion instead of learned heterogeneous GNN message passing. On Qwen2.5-3B, Label Propagation moves the baseline that trains on the 20% labeled rollouts and discards the rest, R1-p, from 75.67 to 75.97 (scores are overall test results in percent on 10 benchmarks). MemReward reaches 77.02, 1.05 points above Label Propagation, and the largest differences appear on the MATH and GPQA benchmarks. So the gains of MemReward cannot be explained by label propagation alone.

Why not skip the graph and take the missing rewards from the policy's confidence?

To isolate whether the improvement of MemReward comes from graph-based propagation rather than from simply assigning additional unverified rewards, called pseudo-rewards, we add a FixMatch-style baseline. Using the same 20% labeled data, this baseline estimates the confidence of each unlabeled rollout from the average probability the policy gives the answer part of its output, keeps only the 30% most confident unlabeled rollouts in each batch and assigns them pseudo-rewards, with no graph structure or cross-query information. It scores 72.55 on Qwen2.5-3B, 3.12 points below R1-p, so simply adding high-confidence pseudo-rewards does not explain the gains of MemReward; in this setting, the confidence of the policy does not track closely enough how often it is right, that is, it is not sufficiently calibrated to provide reliable reward supervision.

Why freeze the GNN after warmup instead of retraining it during online policy optimization?

Freezing the GNN is an intentional design choice that prevents a moving-target feedback loop between the reward predictor and the policy: with retraining, each GNN update would change the rewards the policy learns from, and each policy update would change the rollouts the GNN learns from. The 20% ground-truth rewards continuously give policy optimization reliable supervision. Empirically, on Qwen2.5-3B the frozen GNN maintains stable prediction accuracy throughout all 410 training steps, and together with the reward-flip experiment in the Mixed-reward GRPO stage, these results provide no evidence of errors in the predicted rewards building up progressively under our experimental setting.

During online policy optimization, unlabeled queries are not in the warmup graph. Each one is encoded with the same encoder, connected to its top-k most similar warmup queries, and propagated through the trained GNN, which predicts the rewards of its rollouts.

Why can the GNN score a query from a domain it never saw in training?

When labeled and unlabeled queries are randomly partitioned from the same datasets, the setting primarily evaluates whether rewards propagate from a limited labeled subset to unlabeled queries drawn from the same underlying distribution. To evaluate transfer beyond these partitions, we conduct stricter bidirectional cross-domain experiments in which the GNN receives no reward labels from the target domain: we train it only on question answering (QA) datasets and evaluate it on unseen math datasets, then reverse the setting. Compared with a plain neural network that has the same input features and training procedure but no graph structure, the MLP, the GNN is 12.95 accuracy points better for QA to math and 7.35 points better for math to QA, which shows that the graph contains transferable relational information even when the target domain is entirely unseen during GNN training. Although the GNN never observes reward labels from the domain where the policy trains, its rewards improve the resulting policy in both directions: from R1-p's 77.36 to 80.79 on math and from R1-p's 73.95 to 76.80 on QA.

When a query's neighbors disagree or point the wrong way, how does the GNN handle them?

MemReward does not assume that semantically similar queries must always share the same reward pattern. The sentence encoder is used only to construct a candidate query neighborhood, which may contain both reward-consistent and reward-inconsistent experiences, and the GNN then learns reward-relevant aggregation over it. In a representative case with mixed neighbors on Qwen2.5-3B with 20% labels, the seven nearest neighbors have between 0 and 5 of their 8 rollouts correct, with conflicting evidence among neighbors from the same domain and from another, so neither semantic similarity nor same-domain membership alone reliably determines the reward. Despite this mixed neighborhood, the GNN assigns scores of 0.58–0.77 to the five correct rollouts and 0.009–0.048 to the three incorrect ones, and it correctly matches all eight ground-truth labels. This shows that the GNN does not behave as naive nearest-neighbor voting, a majority vote over the nearest labeled queries called kNN, or as a plain average of neighbor labels: it learns weighted aggregation jointly from the query, thinking and answer nodes, which lets it distinguish correct from incorrect rollouts even when nearby neighbors exhibit opposing reward patterns.

GRPO generates rollouts for each query. Labeled rollouts receive their ground-truth reward, and the rest receive the GNN prediction, thresholded at 0.5, or the predicted score itself for continuous rewards. The policy is then updated with the GRPO objective.

Why can GRPO still improve the policy when many predicted rewards are wrong?

GRPO does not require a perfectly calibrated reward classifier: it primarily uses rewards to estimate the relative advantage of the eight rollouts generated for the same query (reward minus group mean, divided by group standard deviation). An imperfect but positively correlated reward signal can therefore still provide useful optimization guidance, especially compared with discarding the 80% unlabeled rollouts entirely. To quantify this on Qwen2.5-1.5B, we randomly flip each GNN-predicted reward and never corrupt the 20% ground-truth rewards: on top of the GNN's natural prediction errors, the overall score on 10 benchmarks falls by 0.72 points at a flip probability of 30% and by 1.01 points at 50%, where the predicted-reward portion is effectively randomized, yet the policy retains much of its performance. We attribute this tolerance to two aspects of the training design: the uncorrupted 20% ground-truth rewards continuously give policy optimization reliable supervision, and GRPO's normalized, group-relative advantages reduce the influence of isolated reward mistakes when the predicted rewards remain positively correlated with the true rewards within each query's group.

Why can 20% labels score higher than full labels on some benchmarks?

R1-Oracle, the Oracle run with ground-truth rewards for all rollouts, is a fully supervised reference rather than a strict per-benchmark upper bound: training still sees limited random samples across several domains, so full reward supervision does not guarantee the best result on every benchmark, particularly when gradients from different domains compete during joint optimization. The selective gains of MemReward on math can be explained by the domain-dependent quality of its predicted rewards: on Qwen2.5-1.5B with 20% labels, the chance that the frozen GNN scores a correct rollout above an incorrect one, its ROC-AUC, is 0.918 on math, 0.871 on QA and 0.754 on code. R1-Oracle supplies uniformly accurate rewards to all domains, so its updates reflect the joint multi-domain objective, whereas in MemReward the uneven reward quality may place relatively greater effective emphasis on math and reduce the influence of noisier gradients from other domains, which can improve math performance beyond R1-Oracle. To test this explanation, we raise the ground-truth ratio for QA and code from 20% to 70%, keep math at 20% and leave the GNN, backbone and GRPO settings unchanged: QA and code improve, while math decreases by 2.01 points and no longer exceeds R1-Oracle. This result is consistent with a multi-task optimization trade-off induced by domain-dependent reward quality.

Performance scales with the label budget

Overall average score on Qwen2.5-3B as the ground-truth label ratio grows. Even at 20% ground-truth labels, MemReward reaches 97.3% of Oracle; at 70%, the gap to Oracle narrows to 0.48 points (99.4% of Oracle).

Beyond Qwen and verifiable rewards

All settings keep ground-truth rewards on only 20% of the training queries. With Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, MemReward improves over R1-p by 2.64 and 3.00 points, reaching 96.1% and 97.8% of R1-Oracle. With Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, it also improves over R1-p when rewards come from an LLM judge on TruthfulQA (+2.86), from human pairwise preferences on Chatbot Arena (+1.67), and from continuous token-level F1 on HotpotQA (+2.68), where the GNN is trained as a regressor.

Each architectural component contributes

The full model consistently outperforms all ablated variants on both model scales across math, QA and code; on 3B it reaches 80.1% in Math, 75.6% in QA and 63.0% in Code. Replacing the GNN with an MLP on query embeddings drops QA to 72.0% on 3B. Removing edge types drops Math to 76.4% on 3B. Removing thinking nodes drops Code to 58.0% on 3B.
