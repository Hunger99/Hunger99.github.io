MemReward: Graph-Based Experience Memory
for LLM Reward Prediction with Limited Labels

Abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning improves large language model (LLM) reasoning using reward signals on sampled rollouts. When reward labels are available for only a subset of rollouts, restricting policy updates to that subset leaves the remaining rollouts unused for policy optimization. Semi-supervised learning propagates labels from labeled to unlabeled samples, but existing methods either diffuse labels with fixed similarity weights or treat each sample independently, ignoring the relations among queries, thinking processes, and answers. We propose MemReward, a graph-based experience memory that propagates rewards to unlabeled rollouts during online policy optimization. MemReward stores queries and the rollouts (thinking processes and final answers) of an initial LLM policy as nodes of a heterogeneous graph connected by similarity and structural edges, and warms up a GNN on labeled rollouts to propagate rewards over this graph. During online RL fine-tuning, unlabeled rollouts are attached to the graph by query similarity, and the policy is updated with ground-truth rewards on labeled rollouts and GNN-predicted rewards on the rest. We evaluate MemReward on 13 benchmarks in mathematics, question answering, and code generation with verifiable rewards, and further with LLM-judge, human-preference, and continuous rewards. With ground-truth rewards on only 20% of rollouts, MemReward achieves 96.6% of the performance of fully supervised training (Oracle) on Qwen2.5-1.5B and 97.3% on Qwen2.5-3B, where it also outperforms label propagation and FixMatch-style self-training. It carries over to Llama backbones up to 8B and closely approaches Oracle on out-of-domain tasks.

For each query with a ground-truth label, across math, question answering and code, the initial policy generates rollouts, each a thinking process and a final answer. The query, thinking process and answer are encoded into embeddings, and the rollouts are stored as experience memory.

Why is the model that turns each text into an embedding, the sentence encoder, kept fixed instead of being trained on rewards?

We keep the sentence encoder fixed because this work asks whether rewards can propagate over neighbors chosen by similarity of meaning, not how to build a better encoder. The main experiments use all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a general-purpose encoder that is not trained on rewards, and they keep it unchanged when the LLM being trained, the backbone, changes from Qwen to Llama, so the Llama experiment changes only the backbone.

The graph has query, thinking and answer nodes, joined by three edge types. Query-query edges link a query to its top-k most similar queries by cosine similarity, query-thinking edges link a query to its thinking nodes, and thinking-answer edges pair each thinking node with its answer. Only query-query edges depend on embedding similarity.

Two similar queries can differ in whether their answers are right. Why connect queries by similarity at all?

Similarity edges are worth keeping because they carry useful but noisy information about rewards. Related queries often share reasoning requirements, solution strategies and failure modes. These edges only propose candidate neighbors: the graph neural network (GNN) can learn to down-weight a neighbor whose rewards disagree.

A thinking text is far longer than its query. Why does that length not skew which experiences get connected?

A thinking text's length or shared wording pattern, its template, cannot change which experiences are connected, because the step that picks neighbors, retrieval, never reads that text. Thinking text acts only after retrieval, in reward prediction and in the step where each node updates its embedding from its neighbors, called message passing. We also measured the length effect directly: longer thinking texts are not systematically more similar to those of their seven nearest neighbors, because the correlation between length and that similarity is −0.287 overall and negative in 7 of 9 per-benchmark measurements.

Why not give every edge a direction and forbid loops, which would make the graph a directed acyclic graph (DAG)?

We leave out that constraint because the graph records relations among experiences, not steps in time, and query similarity gives no single ranking of all queries for a DAG to follow. A DAG could orient query-query edges by generation time or label confidence, but that choice adds a hand-picked rule that the data do not supply, an ordering heuristic. The same choice could also remove the two-way message-passing paths between related experiences and so restrict how information spreads between them.

A heterogeneous GNN aggregates over each edge type with its own learnable weights, then scores each rollout with a scaled dot product between the query and rollout representations. It is trained on labeled rollouts with binary cross-entropy, or with a regression loss for continuous rewards such as token-level F1.

Why train a GNN instead of spreading labels over the same graph with a fixed rule?

We train a GNN because learned message passing gains more from the same graph than a fixed rule does. On Qwen2.5-3B, Label Propagation, which spreads labels over the same query embeddings and similarity graph with a fixed rule, ends 0.30 points above the baseline that trains on the 20% labeled rollouts and discards the rest, R1-p, while MemReward ends 1.35 points above R1-p (scores are test results in percent, averaged over 10 benchmarks). On the held-out test set with the same backbone and 20% labels, the GNN also predicts rewards more accurately than two simpler methods: 83.8%, against 77.5% for a majority vote over the nearest labeled queries, kNN, and 73.5% for a plain neural network with no graph, the MLP.

Why not skip the graph and take the missing rewards from the policy's confidence?

The policy's confidence, the probability it gives its own answer, is not calibrated enough to supply reliable rewards in this setting: it does not track closely enough how often the policy is right. A FixMatch-style baseline keeps only the 30% most confident unlabeled rollouts in each batch and assigns them unverified rewards, pseudo-rewards, using no graph and no information from other queries. This baseline scores 72.55 on Qwen2.5-3B, 3.12 points below R1-p, so adding pseudo-rewards alone does not explain the gain of MemReward.

Why freeze the GNN after warmup instead of retraining it during online policy optimization?

Freezing stops the GNN and the policy from chasing each other: with retraining, each GNN update would change the rewards the policy learns from, and each policy update would change the rollouts the GNN learns from, a moving-target feedback loop. On Qwen2.5-3B, the frozen GNN's accuracy stays stable over the 410 steps of the RL algorithm that updates the policy, GRPO, so it did not fall as the policy changed.

During online policy optimization, unlabeled queries are not in the warmup graph. Each one is encoded with the same encoder, connected to its top-k most similar warmup queries, and propagated through the trained GNN, which predicts the rewards of its rollouts.

Why can the GNN score a query from a domain it never saw in training?

What the GNN learns over similarity edges transfers between math and question answering (QA), in both directions we tested. Queries from a random split of one dataset mostly resemble the labeled ones, so a harder test gives the GNN no labels from the target domain: trained only on QA, the GNN predicts math rewards 12.95 percentage points more accurately than an MLP, and trained only on math, it beats the MLP by 7.35 percentage points on QA. Both models use the same fixed embeddings and training procedure, so this gap comes from the graph. Feeding these predicted rewards to GRPO raises the average over the math benchmarks from R1-p's 77.36 to 80.79 and the average over the QA benchmarks from R1-p's 73.95 to 76.80.

When a query's neighbors disagree or point the wrong way, why can the GNN still score its rollouts correctly?

On one validation query, on Qwen2.5-3B with 20% labels, the seven nearest neighbors had between 0 and 5 of their 8 rollouts correct, in the query's own domain and outside it, so neither similarity nor domain showed which neighbors to trust. The GNN still gave the five correct rollouts scores of 0.58–0.77 and the three incorrect ones 0.009–0.048, which matches all eight labels. The reason is that the GNN is neither kNN voting nor a plain average of neighbor labels: it learns weighted aggregation over the query, thinking and answer nodes, which lets it tell correct rollouts from incorrect ones even when nearby queries show opposing reward patterns.

GRPO generates rollouts for each query. Labeled rollouts receive their ground-truth reward, and the rest receive the GNN prediction, thresholded at 0.5, or the predicted score itself for continuous rewards. The policy is then updated with the GRPO objective.

Why can GRPO still improve the policy when many predicted rewards are wrong?

This tolerance comes from two parts of the training design: the 20% ground-truth rewards stay reliable and keep steering optimization, and GRPO scores each rollout against all eight rollouts of its query (reward minus group mean, divided by group standard deviation), a score called the advantage, so an isolated reward mistake counts for less as long as predictions stay positively related to the true rewards within the group. On Qwen2.5-1.5B, flipping each predicted reward at random, on top of the GNN's natural errors, lowers the 10-benchmark average by 0.72 points at a flip probability of 30% and by 1.01 points at 50%, which leaves 67.09, still above R1-p.

Why can 20% labels score higher than full labels on some benchmarks?

R1-Oracle, the Oracle run trained with all labels, is a reference rather than a per-benchmark upper bound: training still sees limited random samples, and one model learns math, QA and code together, so full labels do not guarantee the best score on every benchmark. For the math benchmarks, one cause is that predicted rewards are most reliable on math: on Qwen2.5-1.5B, the chance that the frozen GNN scores a correct rollout above an incorrect one, its ROC-AUC, is 0.918 on math, 0.871 on QA and 0.754 on code, so MemReward places relatively more emphasis on math and less on the noisier QA and code updates. To test this explanation on the same backbone, we raise the ground-truth ratio for QA and code from 20% to 70% and keep math at 20%: QA and code improve, while math drops by 2.01 points and no longer exceeds R1-Oracle. This result is consistent with a trade-off among domains.

Performance scales with the label budget

Overall average score on Qwen2.5-3B as the ground-truth label ratio grows. Even at 20% ground-truth labels, MemReward reaches 97.3% of Oracle; at 70%, the gap to Oracle narrows to 0.48 points (99.4% of Oracle).

Beyond Qwen and verifiable rewards

All settings keep ground-truth rewards on only 20% of the training queries. With Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, MemReward improves over R1-p by 2.64 and 3.00 points, reaching 96.1% and 97.8% of R1-Oracle. With Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, it also improves over R1-p when rewards come from an LLM judge on TruthfulQA (+2.86), from human pairwise preferences on Chatbot Arena (+1.67), and from continuous token-level F1 on HotpotQA (+2.68), where the GNN is trained as a regressor.

Each architectural component contributes

The full model consistently outperforms all ablated variants on both model scales across math, QA and code; on 3B it reaches 80.1% in Math, 75.6% in QA and 63.0% in Code. Replacing the GNN with an MLP on query embeddings drops QA to 72.0% on 3B. Removing edge types drops Math to 76.4% on 3B. Removing thinking nodes drops Code to 58.0% on 3B.
